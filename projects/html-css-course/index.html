06 / LEARNING DESIGN / UCL COURSEWORK

HTML & CSS for academic staff.

A blended course plan to help academic staff use HTML and CSS with confidence, and apply those skills to their learning content in Canvas.

My role Learner analysis · Course planning · Reflective evaluation

Course design · First episode reviewed by two peers

Duration
20 hours over 3 weeks
Target cohort
20 academic staff
Format
Blended professional learning
Learners & context Course design Peer review Reflection

01 / LEARNERS & CONTEXT

Make code relevant to everyday teaching.

The proposed learners already used Canvas, but were beginners in HTML, CSS and code editors. Their confidence and previous exposure to technology varied. I wanted the design to make room for that difference and connect new skills to a practical goal: greater control over their own teaching materials.

Voluntary professional learning also needs to fit around existing work. Three live workshops offered timely explanation and support, while independent activities allowed practice at the learner’s own pace. Time was included for becoming familiar with the tools before tackling more demanding tasks.

02 / COURSE DESIGN

Build from guided practice to application.

The proposed blend

12 hours liveThree 4-hour Saturday workshops8 hours independentActivities between sessions

Scaffold the move towards independence

Demonstrations and code-along exercises introduce syntax with an immediately visible result in the browser. I used scaffolding to manage cognitive load: examples and guided practice precede more open-ended tasks (Laurillard, 2012). Learners gradually extend a shared “AI in Education” information page, then use that progression as a model for a page on a personally relevant topic and its application in Canvas.

Distinguish practice from production

Even a short piece of HTML produces something visible, so these learning activities overlap. I classified them by purpose: practice develops familiarity with syntax; production applies that knowledge to a defined task. Investigation adds a different demand, such as finding faults in a document and explaining how nesting affects its structure.

Assess more than correct syntax

The plan combines quizzes on syntax and meaning with teacher and peer feedback on learners’ pages. The editor and browser provide intrinsic feedback through the visible results of code; teacher and peer feedback broadens this to structure, layout and alternative solutions. A private reflective blog supports learners in considering their progress and confidence.

03 / PEER REVIEW

Use feedback to question the balance.

Two peers reviewed the first learning episode. They valued the independent learning opportunities and the choice of Slack for support. Their questions about the emphasis on practice, the variety of activities and the sources of feedback prompted specific responses in my reflection.

Peer feedback and the proposed design responses
Peer observation Design response
More investigation Add faulty HTML and CSS documents for learners to analyse before receiving automatic feedback, focusing on syntax, nesting and page structure.
More varied activities Have small groups turn a basic document into a complete page, combining technical decisions with creative choices.
Feedback beyond the editor Use teacher and peer comments, including exchanges in Slack, to discuss layout, structure and the reasoning behind different solutions.
More focused outcomes Refine the number and wording of the outcomes, and clarify what a finished page should demonstrate while allowing individual expression.

I agreed that investigation and collaboration could be stronger, while maintaining that repeated practice and production were essential to learning this subject. The task was to improve the balance and purpose of the activities.

04 / REFLECTION

Explain the choices behind the tools.

Canvas had a dual role: it hosted the activities and was the environment in which learners would apply their skills. CodePen and Sublime offered ways to experiment with code; Slack supported questions and peer exchange. Reflecting on the feedback moved my attention towards how these familiar tools could support more meaningful activities.

The project strengthened my ability to connect learner needs, teaching methods, technology and assessment. A central lesson was that immediate technical feedback is only one part of a learning design: learners also need opportunities to investigate, explain decisions and learn from one another.

Research informing the design

The written rationale drew on these sources, among others:

  • Laurillard (2012), Teaching as a Design Science — learning activities, scaffolding and feedback.
  • Alammary (2019), Blended learning models for introductory programming courses — the blend of live and independent work.
  • Park, Dorn and Forte (2015), An Analysis of HTML and CSS Syntax Errors in a Web Development Course — syntax and nesting.
  • Eiriksdottir and Catrambone (2011), Procedural Instructions, Principles, and Examples — guidance, examples and conceptual explanation.
