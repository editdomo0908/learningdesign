Learning Goal Matcher

Start with what the learner needs to do. Compare ways to teach it—inside and beyond Articulate Storyline.

Selecting a goal updates the methods below.

Learning goals

Use Up and Down arrows to choose a goal, or Home and End to move to the first and last goals. Press Tab to read the selected methods.

01

Recall

Remember facts, terms or rules

When accurate retrieval matters, make the learner bring an answer to mind before revealing it.

Suggested methods

In Articulate Storyline

Built-in features or custom interactions assembled in Storyline.

Knowledge checks
Built-in question slides
  • Example: After a data-security briefing, answer three new questions about handling shared files; each response receives explanatory feedback.

Design guidance Choose question types that match the learning goal. Vary the questions or their order so learners recall the rule, not a memorised sequence.

Recall-first flashcards
Custom interaction
  • Example: Show “What is the first step in requesting annual leave?”; the learner recalls the process before turning the card.

Design guidance Ask learners to answer before they reveal the back of the card. Clicking to see a fact without thinking first is not retrieval practice.

Beyond Storyline

Other activities that may be more authentic or suitable for the goal.

Spaced follow-up questions
LMS / email / flashcard app
  • Example: After onboarding, send short questions about where to find key policies two days, one week and one month later.

Design guidance Send short questions at increasing intervals after training. Use an LMS, flashcard app or messages to revisit knowledge over time.

Teach-back practice
Live / peer activity
  • Example: Ask a colleague to recall the steps for reporting a lost work device without looking at the guidance.

Design guidance Ask for an explanation without notes, then have a peer check whether the important steps or rules were recalled accurately.

Research behind these choices

Retrieval practice and spaced practice can support retention. The evidence concerns the learning process, not a specific flip-card visual. Feedback and later opportunities to retrieve matter.

  • Maye & Hurley, 2026 ↗Spaced repetition meta-analysis (medical education)
  • Gonçalves et al., 2025 ↗Retrieval vs. elaborative learning meta-analysis
  • Ingebrigtsen et al., 2025 ↗Digital flashcards study
  • Cai et al., 2023 ↗Feedback meta-analysis
02

Explain

Understand how or why something works

Ask learners to explain a relationship, not merely click through an explanation.

Suggested methods

In Articulate Storyline

Built-in features or custom interactions assembled in Storyline.

Annotated diagram with a prediction
Built-in markers + custom question
  • Example: Reveal a project handover step by step, then ask why leaving a task without an owner could delay the next team.

Design guidance First ask learners to predict what will happen. Reveal the diagram in stages, and explain why their answer fits or not.

Pause-and-explain video
Video + question slides
  • Example: Pause a short file-sharing demo before access is granted: “Why was view-only access chosen?”

Design guidance Pause just before the important event. Ask learners to explain what should happen next, then provide the reasoned answer.

Beyond Storyline

Other activities that may be more authentic or suitable for the goal.

Teach-back
Conversation / live class
  • Example: Explain how agendas and action notes help a team run meetings; the listener asks one “why” question.

Design guidance Ask learners to explain the idea in their own words. A facilitator should probe unclear reasoning and help correct misunderstandings.

Concept mapping
Paper / whiteboard tool
  • Example: Connect a project’s goals, tasks, owners and deadlines on a map; explain how a change in one affects the others.

Design guidance Ask learners to draw connections, then compare them with an expert map. Discuss missing or incorrect links and revise the map.

Research behind these choices

Signalling can guide attention to relevant visual information; active questions during video may help comprehension and transfer. Neither study proves that clicking hotspots creates understanding.

  • Schneider et al., 2018 ↗Visual signalling in multimedia meta-analysis
  • Zhang et al., 2025 ↗Active strategies in video learning meta-analysis
  • Cai et al., 2023 ↗Feedback meta-analysis
03

Apply

Use a procedure in a new situation

Provide an example and then let the learner use the procedure on a fresh task.

Suggested methods

In Articulate Storyline

Built-in features or custom interactions assembled in Storyline.

Guided software simulation
Built-in screen recording
  • Example: Practise submitting an expense claim in a simulated staff portal, with guidance for each step.

Design guidance Start with guided steps in Storyline Try mode, then remove hints in a later attempt so learners perform independently.

Worked example → new case
Custom slides + quiz
  • Example: Study how a sample expense claim follows the company policy, then complete a claim for a different trip.

Design guidance Model how to solve the first case, then give a different but comparable case. Feedback should explain how the same rule applies.

Beyond Storyline

Other activities that may be more authentic or suitable for the goal.

Sandbox practice
Real or test system
  • Example: Submit a sample expense claim in a test version of the staff portal, using fictional receipts.

Design guidance Use a safe test environment when the goal is genuine software use. Let learners practise without changing live company data.

Supervised job task
Workplace coaching
  • Example: Prepare a routine meeting invitation while a colleague observes and gives feedback.

Design guidance Observe the task against a short checklist. Give targeted feedback and another chance to complete it correctly.

Research behind these choices

Worked examples plus problem solving can support learning, depending on sequence and experience. Authentic tasks are more defensible tests of real-world application than course completion alone.

  • Zeitlhofer & Zumbach, 2026 ↗Sequencing examples and problem solving
  • Koçoğlu & Kanadlı, 2025 ↗Problem-based learning second-order meta-analysis
  • Cai et al., 2023 ↗Feedback meta-analysis
04

Decide

Choose between possible actions

Make the consequences and reasoning visible, especially when there is no simple yes/no answer.

Suggested methods

In Articulate Storyline

Built-in features or custom interactions assembled in Storyline.

Branching decision scenario
Custom layers / triggers / variables
  • Example: A colleague asks you to share a confidential file for a meeting. Choose how to respond and see what happens next.

Design guidance Make each option realistic and show its consequence. Explain why the chosen action works or fails in that situation.

Scenario-based multiple choice
Built-in quiz + feedback
  • Example: Two deadlines clash. Choose the most suitable next action and compare your reasoning with the feedback.

Design guidance Write believable alternatives rather than obvious wrong answers. Explain the reasoning behind the best choice in the feedback.

Beyond Storyline

Other activities that may be more authentic or suitable for the goal.

Role-play with feedback
Live practice
  • Example: One person requests an urgent task when workloads are already full; the other decides how to negotiate priorities, then receives feedback.

Design guidance Evaluate both the chosen action and how it is communicated. Offer specific feedback and repeat the conversation if needed.

Case conference
Group discussion
  • Example: Discuss three ways to cover a teammate’s planned absence and justify one using workload and deadlines.

Design guidance Use cases with genuine trade-offs. Ask participants to explain their assumptions and compare reasonable alternatives.

Research behind these choices

Simulation studies show benefits in some settings, while the small workplace branching study mainly documents engagement. Evidence is not proof that every branching Storyline scenario improves work decisions.

  • Görücü et al., 2024 ↗Simulation and clinical decision-making meta-analysis
  • Pimblett et al., 2026 ↗Workplace branching-scenario case study
  • Cai et al., 2023 ↗Feedback meta-analysis
05

Diagnose

Identify errors, causes or patterns

Give enough evidence to investigate, then require an explanation of what went wrong.

Suggested methods

In Articulate Storyline

Built-in features or custom interactions assembled in Storyline.

Find-the-error task
Built-in hotspot question
  • Example: Inspect an expense form and identify the missing information that caused it to be returned.

Design guidance Use a realistic error and ask learners to locate it before revealing the answer. Explain what caused it and how to fix it.

Compare contrasting examples
Custom layers / triggers
  • Example: Compare a clear meeting invitation with a confusing one; identify the missing details and explain why they matter.

Design guidance Choose contrasting examples that differ in a meaningful way. Ask learners to identify the clues that distinguish them.

Beyond Storyline

Other activities that may be more authentic or suitable for the goal.

Sandbox troubleshooting
Live system / test environment
  • Example: Investigate why a sample shared folder cannot be opened, testing possible causes in a safe practice workspace.

Design guidance Allow learners to test more than one possible cause. Use evidence from each attempt to decide what to try next.

Peer error review
Collaborative review
  • Example: Read a fictional handover in which a task was missed; critique a colleague’s explanation using evidence from the handover.

Design guidance Ask learners to point to the evidence for each suspected error and suggest a corrective action, rather than just naming a fault.

Research behind these choices

Error-based learning can help, but unguided search can overload novices. Matching tasks and hints to prior knowledge matters more than the visual interaction type.

  • Dieterich et al., 2025 ↗Learning from erroneous examples systematic review
  • Koçoğlu & Kanadlı, 2025 ↗Problem-based learning second-order meta-analysis
  • Cai et al., 2023 ↗Feedback meta-analysis
06

Connect

Understand relationships between elements

The goal is to represent connections, dependencies or structures—not simply explore hidden content.

Suggested methods

In Articulate Storyline

Built-in features or custom interactions assembled in Storyline.

Interactive process map
Custom markers / layers
  • Example: Explore how HR, IT and a manager prepare for a new starter; explain why one task depends on another.

Design guidance Ask learners to predict what happens when one part of the process changes. Use the map to explain dependencies, not just to reveal labels.

Sort and group relationships
Built-in drag-and-drop quiz
  • Example: Match everyday tasks—booking leave, requesting software and ordering equipment—to the teams responsible, then discuss ambiguous cases.

Design guidance Explain the grouping rule before the activity. After submission, show why each item belongs in its group.

Beyond Storyline

Other activities that may be more authentic or suitable for the goal.

Build a concept map
Whiteboard / mapping software
  • Example: Map how a project’s goals, tasks, owners and deadlines depend on one another; explain the links you chose.

Design guidance Let learners move ideas and connections freely, then revise their map after comparing it with a model or peer feedback.

Collaborative workflow mapping
Facilitated workshop
  • Example: HR, IT and team managers map the onboarding process together and identify different assumptions about responsibilities.

Design guidance Use when teams need a shared understanding. Discuss conflicting assumptions before finalising the workflow map.

Research behind these choices

Multimedia signalling supports attention to relevant relationships. Claims that mapping itself improves learning depend on how the learner constructs and explains connections; these studies are indirect.

  • Schneider et al., 2018 ↗Visual signalling in multimedia meta-analysis
  • Koçoğlu & Kanadlı, 2025 ↗Problem-based learning second-order meta-analysis
07

Perform

Complete a procedure or routine

Use guided rehearsal initially, then remove the prompts and assess on a representative task.

Suggested methods

In Articulate Storyline

Built-in features or custom interactions assembled in Storyline.

Try-mode software simulation
Built-in screen recording
  • Example: Practise every step of booking annual leave in a simulated staff portal, using hints only when needed.

Design guidance Use Try mode for guided practice. Once learners can follow the process, use Test mode for an independent attempt.

Scenario rehearsal with feedback
Custom triggers / quiz slides
  • Example: Rehearse a project handover before annual leave, receiving feedback on each decision about notes, files and task ownership.

Design guidance Break the task into realistic decisions. Give feedback at each stage so learners can adjust their next action.

Beyond Storyline

Other activities that may be more authentic or suitable for the goal.

Practice in the real environment
Test system / workplace
  • Example: Use a practice account in a meeting tool to schedule a meeting, invite attendees and share an agenda without prompts.

Design guidance Choose a sandbox or test account when possible. Realistic practice exposes system behaviours that slides may not reproduce.

Observed demonstration
Manager / coach
  • Example: Demonstrate how to set up a hybrid meeting while a colleague checks audio, screen sharing and participant access.

Design guidance Provide an observable success checklist. Give feedback on specific steps and allow a second demonstration.

Research behind these choices

Simulation and feedback can support practice, but transfer needs checking in the actual task environment. A simulated completion score is not the same as demonstrated workplace competence.

  • Görücü et al., 2024 ↗Simulation and clinical decision-making meta-analysis
  • Chernikova et al., 2025 ↗Adaptive simulation meta-analysis
  • Cai et al., 2023 ↗Feedback meta-analysis
08

Evaluate

Judge alternatives against criteria

Make the criteria explicit and ask learners to justify their judgement using evidence.

Suggested methods

In Articulate Storyline

Built-in features or custom interactions assembled in Storyline.

Option comparison with criteria
Custom layers / variables
  • Example: Compare three onboarding plans for accessibility, time and cost; recommend one and explain the trade-offs.

Design guidance Show the criteria before presenting the options. Ask learners to justify their choice using the evidence for each criterion.

Judgement case with rationale
Built-in quiz + feedback
  • Example: Compare two workshop proposals, then check your recommendation against an expert’s criteria for relevance, accessibility and cost.

Design guidance After a learner makes a choice, reveal an expert rubric or worked example so they can compare their reasoning.

Beyond Storyline

Other activities that may be more authentic or suitable for the goal.

Structured debate
Workshop / seminar
  • Example: Two groups defend different ways to run team meetings using the same criteria for participation, preparation time and clear actions.

Design guidance Give both sides the same evidence standards and evaluation criteria. Ask them to challenge claims with reasons, not preference.

Authentic document review
Shared document / peer review
  • Example: Score two project-handover plans for completeness, clear responsibilities and usable files; write a recommendation.

Design guidance Keep the document editable so participants can mark evidence, discuss changes and improve the original work.

Research behind these choices

Problem-based learning and explanatory feedback provide relevant but indirect support. These papers do not establish a unique advantage for Storyline comparison interfaces.

  • Koçoğlu & Kanadlı, 2025 ↗Problem-based learning second-order meta-analysis
  • Cai et al., 2023 ↗Feedback meta-analysis
09

Create

Produce and revise original work

A course can prepare someone to create, but the finished artefact usually belongs in a real working tool.

Suggested methods

In Articulate Storyline

Built-in features or custom interactions assembled in Storyline.

Scaffolded design brief
Custom guided slides
  • Example: Review a sample welcome guide, then plan a one-page guide for new starters using a brief and checklist.

Design guidance Use examples and a checklist to help learners plan their solution. Let them create the final artefact in a suitable production tool.

Prototype and reflection prompts
Custom text entry / feedback
  • Example: Sketch a project-handover template and explain how each section helps the next person take over; compare your design with the guidance.

Design guidance Use Storyline text entry for short ideas and reflection. For a substantial prototype, move to a tool designed for creating and editing.

Beyond Storyline

Other activities that may be more authentic or suitable for the goal.

Make a real artefact
Figma / document / authoring tool
  • Example: Create a one-page welcome guide for new starters in a document or slide editor and submit the editable file.

Design guidance Use a tool suited to the output, such as a document editor for a guide or a slide editor for a presentation. Assess the produced work.

Iterate through peer critique
Review session / collaboration
  • Example: Share a first draft of a handover template, receive feedback against agreed criteria and revise it before trying it with a teammate.

Design guidance Set clear review criteria, then require a revision. Compare the first and second versions to make the learning visible.

Research behind these choices

Problem-based learning and useful feedback support this direction broadly. No study listed here proves that a Storyline interaction alone develops creative production skill.

  • Koçoğlu & Kanadlı, 2025 ↗Problem-based learning second-order meta-analysis
  • Cai et al., 2023 ↗Feedback meta-analysis
  • Zeitlhofer & Zumbach, 2026 ↗Sequencing examples and problem solving

02 / SOURCES

Further reading

Research supports learning processes, not a specific slide layout or widget.

Browse all 13 studies
Systematic review + meta-analysis · medical education

The Effectiveness of Spaced Repetition in Medical Education: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Maye & Hurley (2026)

13 studies in meta-analysis; 21,415 learners. Spaced repetition favoured over conventional study (SMD 0.78), but this is medical-education evidence and does not establish effectiveness of an unspaced flip-card interaction.

Read paper ↗
Meta-analysis · comparative learning methods

Retrieval Practice Versus Elaborative Encoding: A Systematic and Meta-analytic Review

Gonçalves, Muniz & Jaeger (2025)

44 studies / 142 comparisons. Retrieval had a small overall advantage (g 0.14) over elaborative methods; advantage depended on feedback, and other effective methods often performed similarly.

Read paper ↗
Quasi-experimental study · nursing students

Effective, Scalable, and Low Cost: The Use of Teacher-Made Digital Flashcards Improves Student Learning

Ingebrigtsen et al. (2025)

799 first-year nursing students at 19 campuses. Flashcard users scored higher on exams (d 0.42), but uptake was only about one-third, and user-vs-nonuser differences cannot establish a fully randomised causal effect.

Read paper ↗
Meta-analysis · technology-rich environments

The effect of feedback on academic achievement in technology-rich learning environments: A meta-analytic review

Cai et al. (2023)

61 studies / 182 effects. Feedback improved achievement versus no feedback (g 0.44); explanatory feedback was strongest among the types considered.

Read paper ↗
Meta-analysis · video-based learning

Active learning strategies in video learning: A meta-analysis

Zhang et al. (2025)

54 studies. Active strategies versus passive watching improved retention (g 0.33), comprehension (g 0.28) and transfer (g 0.43), but also slightly increased cognitive load (g 0.19). Evidence heavily represents adults and STEM.

Read paper ↗
Systematic review · error-based learning

Conditions for Effective Learning from Erroneous Examples: A Systematic Review

Dieterich, Rumann & Rodemer (2025)

40 studies. Finding and explaining errors can help, but effects depend on learner knowledge, guidance and complexity; prompts can also cause overload.

Read paper ↗
Meta-analysis · nursing education

The effect of simulation-based learning on nursing students’ clinical decision-making skills: Systematic review and meta-analysis

Görücü, Türk & Karaçam (2024)

14 included studies indicated improvement in clinical decision-making from simulation. Transfer to corporate branching scenarios is a reasoned design analogy, not directly tested here.

Read paper ↗
Qualitative action case study · workplace training

Enhancing workplace e-learning with branching scenarios: An action case study

Pimblett, Rowe & Fenton (2026)

10 adult learners / 30 interviews. Authentic choices and timely feedback were linked to engagement; this small study does not demonstrate better retention or job performance causally.

Read paper ↗
Second-order meta-analysis · problem-based learning

The effect of problem-based learning approach on learning outcomes: A second-order meta-analysis study

Koçoğlu & Kanadlı (2025)

22 prior meta-analyses / 36 datasets; combined effect g 0.726. Studies of lower quality showed larger effects. This evaluates instructional approaches, not a specific Storyline case-study slide.

Read paper ↗
Meta-analysis · multimedia signalling

A meta-analysis of how signaling affects learning with media

Schneider et al. (2018)

103 studies / 12,201 participants. Highlighting relevant visual information improved retention (g 0.53) and transfer (g 0.33). Evidence supports cues, not clicking hotspots for their own sake.

Read paper ↗
Meta-analysis · educational gamification

Gamification enhances student intrinsic motivation, perceptions of autonomy and relatedness, but minimal impact on competency

Li, Hew & Du (2024)

35 interventions / 2,500 learners. Small effect on intrinsic motivation (g 0.257), limited effect on perceived competence; badges and points are not a substitute for practice.

Read paper ↗
Experimental study · mathematics

Sequencing problem solving and worked examples: effects on performance, cognitive load, and judgments of learning

Zeitlhofer & Zumbach (2026)

124 participants learning equations. Sequences including problem solving outperformed worked-example-only training; results cannot be assumed to generalise to all professional tasks.

Read paper ↗
Meta-analysis · simulation-based learning

Personalization through adaptivity or adaptability? A meta-analysis on simulation-based learning in higher education

Chernikova et al. (2025)

Suggests the value of adapting simulations to learner needs and prior knowledge. This is not evidence that every adaptive Storyline branch produces better learning.

Read paper ↗

Most studies investigate teaching strategies rather than Articulate Storyline. Populations, tasks and comparisons differ; some mappings here are design judgements, not directly tested effects.
