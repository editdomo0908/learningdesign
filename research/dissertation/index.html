UCL Institute of Education / MA Education and Technology

How authoring tools shape design.

A study of instructional designers’ software choices, working methods and decision-making. The dissertation received UCL’s Exceptional Dissertation Award.

The question

E-learning authoring tools are often described as neutral means of producing a course. My dissertation asked how the tools themselves influence instructional designers’ choices, what motivates the selection of a particular tool, and which constraints designers encounter.

Research approach

The qualitative research combined an online questionnaire with semi-structured interviews: 22 questionnaire responses and four interviews. It considered why designers choose certain tools and how software features and limitations influence their approach.

These are exploratory qualitative findings from a small, non-representative sample. They should not be treated as industry-wide percentages or causal effects.

Selected findings

01 / Tool influence

Tools guide what feels possible

Built-in interactions, features and limitations influenced design choices, rather than merely implementing plans made independently of the software.

02 / Market influence

Selection is not purely pedagogical

Tool preference, including demand for Articulate products, was shaped by client preferences and market expectations.

03 / Designer agency

Convenience creates a trade-off

The same software that speeds production may narrow the range of design approaches considered. The implication is to evaluate the tool critically.

04 / Evaluation

Questions after delivery

Interviewed designers reported limited follow-up with clients about course effectiveness. This does not establish whether clients conducted their own evaluation.

Across these themes, I became interested in where decisions originate: from learners' needs, from designers’ professional judgement, or from the affordances of the tool selected.

What this means for learning design

Templates and features can be useful starting points, but they should not define the learning problem. The sequence matters: identify the outcome, choose how to practise and assess it, then select the software capable of supporting that design.

This question continues in the Learning Interaction Selector, which maps interaction patterns to learning goals and cites peer-reviewed instructional research.
