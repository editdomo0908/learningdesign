Essay / AI and learning design · Draft for review

AI, white noise and avatars.

What happens when producing learning content becomes easier than deciding whether it is worth producing?

When content becomes noise

It is increasingly easy to produce a learning video: a script, a synthetic voice, an animated presenter, a few slides. The output can look finished long before anyone has established what a learner should be able to do afterwards.

Here, “AI white noise” describes a potential problem of volume and repetition: more explanations, summaries and media competing for attention without adding much understanding. This is a working metaphor, not a scientific term or a diagnosis of all AI-produced material.

A 2025 mixed-methods study of science-education videos screened 1,082 clips from YouTube and TikTok and classified 57 (5.3%) as probably low-quality AI-generated material. The authors identified problems such as misleading explanations and disorganised presentation. The finding supports caution in that sample; it does not establish how prevalent poor AI content is across all learning platforms. Study ↗

What is the avatar doing?

A presenter can create social cues, draw attention to relevant information or offer guidance. Equally, a talking face can occupy screen space while repeating material the learner could read more quickly.

The evidence is more nuanced than “avatars work” or “avatars distract”. A 2025 meta-analysis of 33 experimental studies found positive effects of social cues in AI-powered pedagogical agents on cognitive, affective and behavioural outcomes; it found no statistically detectable overall effect on cognitive load. Another 2025 meta-analysis, covering 24 studies, found a small or negligible overall reduction in cognitive load from pedagogical agents. These studies encompass different forms of agents, not only synthetic video presenters. Social-cues review ↗ · Cognitive-load review ↗

For instructional design, the useful question is what the avatar contributes to the learning task. It might guide a novice through a complex procedure, model a conversation or give feedback. Simply reading bullet points aloud is a much weaker justification.

Design questions before production

  • Is the learning goal to recognise, explain, practise or decide?
  • What does the spoken or visual element add that text or a demonstration does not?
  • Will learners have to retrieve, apply or evaluate anything?
  • Could removing the avatar make the material easier to follow?
  • How will learning be checked after the content is viewed?

A 2025 research article on generative AI and load-reduction instruction argues for scaffolding, feedback, structured practice and independent problem-solving. It is a theoretical instructional framework, not a trial proving that a particular production format is superior. Read the paper ↗

Production is not instructional design

AI-generated video and synthetic presenters have legitimate uses, including rapid revisions, multilingual versions and scenarios where an actor is impractical. The risk appears when visual polish is used as a proxy for educational value.

Technology changes what is easy to produce. Designers still have to decide what deserves to be taught, what practice is necessary, and which parts of the experience should demand the learner's own thinking.

Editorial status: working article draft. The term “AI white noise” is used here as a metaphor; cited findings should not be read as proof that every AI video or avatar harms learning.
